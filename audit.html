IT Auditor

IT auditors evaluate an organization's technology systems, processes, and controls to determine whether they are secure, reliable, compliant, and operating as intended — sitting at the intersection of information systems, cybersecurity, and business governance.


Career Overview

IT auditors evaluate an organization's technology systems, processes, and controls to determine whether they are secure, reliable, compliant, and operating as intended. Rather than primarily building technology, IT auditors examine how technology is being used and whether appropriate safeguards are in place. Their work sits at the intersection of information systems, cybersecurity, accounting, risk management, and business governance. IT auditors may review access controls, system changes, data protection, backup procedures, cybersecurity practices, and other controls that help organizations manage technology-related risks.


What You'll Do

An IT auditor begins by identifying the systems, processes, and risks relevant to an audit. They may interview employees, examine policies and documentation, review system configurations, test controls, analyze data, and collect evidence to determine whether controls are functioning effectively. Auditors document their findings and communicate weaknesses or risks to management, often recommending specific improvements. Depending on the organization, they may also evaluate compliance with laws, regulations, industry standards, or internal policies. IT auditors must maintain detailed documentation and approach their work objectively and systematically.


Skills

IT auditors need a strong combination of technology, business, analytical, and communication skills. Technical knowledge may include databases, operating systems, networking, cybersecurity, access management, cloud systems, and information security controls. Business knowledge in accounting, risk management, internal controls, governance, and compliance is also valuable. Auditors must be highly detail-oriented and comfortable examining evidence, identifying inconsistencies, documenting conclusions, and communicating findings professionally. Familiarity with data analysis tools, spreadsheets, SQL, and audit or governance frameworks can strengthen an IT auditor's skill set.


Education

A bachelor's degree in information systems, accounting, cybersecurity, business, or a related field can prepare students for IT auditing. Information Systems is especially relevant because it provides students with an understanding of both technology and business processes. Coursework in accounting, information security, databases, systems analysis, risk management, and internal controls can be useful preparation. Professional certifications may also become valuable as an auditor's career develops. Relevant experience through internships in audit, consulting, cybersecurity, or technology can help students demonstrate practical knowledge.


Career Path

Entry-level professionals may begin as IT audit associates, technology risk associates, internal audit analysts, or consulting associates. With experience, they can advance to senior IT auditor, IT audit manager, technology risk manager, or internal audit manager. Professionals may also specialize in areas such as cybersecurity auditing, cloud controls, compliance, privacy, or technology risk. Experienced IT auditors can move into broader risk-management, governance, cybersecurity, consulting, or executive roles.


Example Projects

An IT auditing student might perform a mock audit of an organization's user-access controls, evaluating whether employees have appropriate permissions and whether former employees' accounts are properly removed. Other projects could include reviewing a fictional company's backup and disaster-recovery controls, evaluating cybersecurity policies, testing database access controls, or creating an IT risk assessment. A comprehensive project could involve identifying risks, documenting existing controls, designing audit tests, analyzing evidence, writing findings, and presenting recommendations to management.


Common Employers

IT auditors commonly work for public accounting and consulting firms, internal audit departments, financial institutions, government organizations, healthcare companies, technology companies, and large corporations. Consulting and accounting firms may employ IT auditors to perform technology-risk assessments for multiple clients, while internal auditors typically focus on the systems and controls of a single organization. Industries with significant regulatory requirements or large amounts of sensitive information often have substantial demand for technology-risk and audit professionals.
