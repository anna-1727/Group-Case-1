Security Analyst

Security analysts protect an organization's information, systems, networks, and users from cybersecurity threats — combining technical knowledge with analytical problem-solving to identify vulnerabilities and strengthen defenses.


Career Overview

Security analysts protect an organization's information, systems, networks, and users from cybersecurity threats. They combine technical knowledge with analytical problem-solving to identify vulnerabilities, monitor for suspicious activity, investigate security incidents, and strengthen an organization's defenses. Security analysts may work within an organization's IT or cybersecurity department or as part of a consulting firm. The role is especially important as organizations increasingly depend on cloud services, connected systems, artificial intelligence, and digital information. For Information Systems students, security analysis provides an opportunity to apply both technical and business knowledge to protect valuable organizational resources.


What You'll Do

A security analyst's responsibilities can vary depending on the organization, but commonly include monitoring networks and systems for suspicious activity, investigating potential security breaches, identifying vulnerabilities, maintaining security tools, and developing security policies and procedures. Analysts may also help conduct risk assessments, document incidents, recommend improvements to management, and participate in disaster recovery planning. Because cybersecurity threats constantly evolve, security analysts must regularly research new vulnerabilities, attack techniques, and defensive technologies. The role often involves collaboration with network administrators, software developers, systems analysts, managers, and other employees.


Skills

Successful security analysts need a combination of technical, analytical, and communication skills. Technical knowledge may include networking, operating systems, databases, cloud environments, authentication, encryption, vulnerability management, and security monitoring. Analysts also benefit from familiarity with security tools and scripting or programming languages. Equally important are analytical thinking, attention to detail, problem-solving, and communication skills. Security professionals must be able to investigate technical problems and then explain their findings clearly to people who may not have a technical background.


Education

A bachelor's degree in information technology, information systems, computer science, cybersecurity, or a related field is a common starting point for a career in security analysis. BYU's Information Systems program is particularly relevant because it combines technical skills with an understanding of organizational needs and business processes. Coursework in networking, databases, programming, cybersecurity, systems analysis, and data can provide a strong foundation. Internships, hands-on projects, and industry certifications can further demonstrate practical skills to employers. Some security analysts begin their careers in related IT positions before specializing in cybersecurity.


Career Path

Many security professionals begin in entry-level roles such as security analyst, cybersecurity analyst, security operations center analyst, or related IT positions. With experience, they may specialize in areas such as incident response, vulnerability management, security engineering, digital forensics, or cloud security. Experienced analysts can progress into senior analyst and security engineer positions or move toward leadership roles such as security manager, cybersecurity director, or chief information security officer. The field also provides opportunities to move between technical, consulting, risk, and management positions.


Example Projects

An Information Systems student interested in security could build a home cybersecurity monitoring lab, analyze sample network logs for suspicious activity, conduct a vulnerability assessment of a controlled test environment, or create a security-awareness program for a fictional organization. Other projects might include developing a password-security assessment tool, documenting an incident-response plan, analyzing a simulated security breach, or creating a dashboard that tracks security events. Projects like these demonstrate the ability to combine technical investigation with documentation, risk analysis, and communication.


Common Employers

Security analysts are needed across nearly every industry because organizations of all types depend on technology and digital information. Common employers include technology companies, financial institutions, healthcare organizations, government agencies, consulting firms, universities, retailers, manufacturers, and large corporations with dedicated cybersecurity teams. The BLS reports that major employment sectors for information security analysts include computer systems design, finance and insurance, management of companies, information, and professional consulting services.
